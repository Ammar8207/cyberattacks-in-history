# Foundations: Understanding Cyberattacks

## Introduction

Before we explore the major cyberattacks that have shaped our digital world, we need to establish a common vocabulary. Cyberattacks vary widely in their methods, targets, and impact. Whether you're new to cybersecurity or have some experience, this chapter will provide clear definitions and context for understanding the concepts we'll encounter throughout this book.

We'll define what cyberattacks are, explore the major categories, identify who launches them and why, and discuss the fundamentals that make these attacks possible.

## What Is a Cyberattack?

A **cyberattack** is any malicious attempt to damage, disrupt, or gain unauthorized access to a computer system, network, or digital device. Cyberattacks range from simple password guessing to sophisticated, coordinated efforts targeting critical infrastructure.

Key characteristics of cyberattacks:

- **Intentional** — They are deliberate actions, not accidents
- **Unauthorized** — They bypass normal security controls or exploit vulnerabilities
- **Harmful** — They cause damage, theft, or disruption
- **Digital** — They occur in cyberspace (networks, computers, software)

Cyberattacks can target individuals, organizations, governments, or entire infrastructure systems like power grids and hospitals.

## Types of Cyberattacks

Understanding the different attack categories helps us recognize patterns in history and anticipate future threats.

### Malware

**Malware** is software designed to harm, exploit, or gain unauthorized access to a system. Types include:

- **Viruses** — Self-replicating code that spreads through infected files
- **Worms** — Self-spreading malware that replicates across networks without user action
- **Trojans** — Programs that appear legitimate but contain hidden malicious code
- **Ransomware** — Encrypts files and demands payment for decryption
- **Spyware** — Secretly monitors user activity and steals information
- **Rootkits** — Hide malicious code deep in the operating system to maintain access

### Phishing

**Phishing** is a social engineering attack where attackers impersonate trusted entities to trick people into revealing sensitive information or clicking malicious links.

Common phishing variants:

- **Email phishing** — Fraudulent emails appearing to come from legitimate companies
- **Spear phishing** — Targeted attacks using personalized information about the victim
- **Whaling** — Phishing attacks specifically targeting high-level executives
- **Vishing** — Phone-based phishing using voice calls

### Denial of Service (DoS) and Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS)

**DoS attacks** flood a system with traffic or requests to overwhelm it and make it unavailable to legitimate users. A **DDoS attack** uses many computers (often compromised) to launch the attack simultaneously, making it harder to block.

Common methods:

- **Volumetric attacks** — Massive amounts of traffic flood the target
- **Protocol attacks** — Exploitation of network protocols (like SYN floods)
- **Application-layer attacks** — Targeting specific applications or services

### Man-in-the-Middle (MitM)

An attacker intercepts communication between two parties, potentially eavesdropping or modifying messages without either party knowing. These attacks are especially dangerous on unsecured networks.

### SQL Injection

Attackers insert malicious code into input fields to manipulate databases. This can allow unauthorized access to sensitive data or system control.

### Zero-Day Exploits

A **zero-day vulnerability** is a security flaw unknown to the software vendor. Attackers who discover it can launch attacks before a patch is available, giving them a significant advantage.

### Password Attacks

- **Brute force** — Trying many password combinations until one works
- **Dictionary attacks** — Using common words and known passwords
- **Credential stuffing** — Using leaked username/password pairs from other breaches
- **Social engineering** — Manipulating people into revealing passwords

### Insider Threats

Attacks from current or former employees, contractors, or partners who have legitimate access but abuse it to steal data, sabotage systems, or sell information.

## Threat Actors and Motivations

Understanding *who* launches cyberattacks and *why* is crucial for recognizing patterns throughout history.

### Types of Threat Actors

**Cybercriminals** are motivated by profit. They steal data, launch ransomware attacks, commit fraud, or sell stolen information on underground markets.

**Hacktivists** are motivated by ideology or social causes. They attack organizations they believe are unethical, often for publicity or to expose wrongdoing.

**Nation-states** (state-sponsored attackers) are government-funded and motivated by espionage, military advantage, or political goals. These attacks tend to be highly sophisticated and well-funded.

**Script kiddies** have limited technical skills and use pre-made tools and scripts created by others. Despite lower sophistication, they can still cause damage.

**Insiders** are employees or contractors with legitimate access who abuse their position for revenge, financial gain, or ideology.

**Competitors** may launch cyberattacks for economic advantage through corporate espionage or sabotage.

### Motivations

| Motivation | Actor Type | Example |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Financial profit | Cybercriminals | Ransomware, data theft |
| Espionage | Nation-states, competitors | Stealing trade secrets, military intel |
| Disruption | Hacktivists, nation-states | Taking down websites, infrastructure damage |
| Revenge | Insiders, disgruntled actors | Stealing data before leaving company |
| Ideology | Hacktivists | Exposing perceived wrongdoing |
| Testing/research | Security researchers | Authorized penetration testing |

## Why Cyberattacks Work: The Vulnerability Triangle

Most successful cyberattacks rely on three factors:

1. **Technical vulnerabilities** — Flaws in software, systems, or networks that can be exploited
2. **Human factors** — Mistakes, poor password practices, or susceptibility to social engineering
3. **Insufficient defenses** — Lack of security measures, monitoring, or incident response

A successful attack typically exploits at least two of these factors. This is why effective cybersecurity requires technical solutions *and* human awareness *and* strong policies.

## The Evolution of Attack Sophistication

Early cyberattacks were often launched by curious individuals or small groups with limited resources. Modern cyberattacks, especially those from nation-states or well-funded criminal organizations, involve:

- Months or years of reconnaissance and planning
- Multiple stages and sophisticated tools
- Careful operational security to hide tracks
- Supply chain compromises to reach difficult targets
- Custom malware designed specifically for the target

## Summary

Cyberattacks are diverse, intentional acts of digital harm. They range from simple phishing emails to sophisticated state-sponsored operations. Understanding the types of attacks, the motivations of threat actors, and the vulnerabilities they exploit is essential for recognizing patterns in history and protecting systems today.

In the chapters ahead, we'll see how these concepts come to life in real incidents that shaped cybersecurity as we know it.
